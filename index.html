Analysis of eating habits

Individual work with a nutritionist

Nutrition is much more than a set of foods, calories, and rules. It's a part of our daily lives, closely linked to habits, daily rhythm, work, rest, family traditions, workload, and even how we organize our time.

That's why we offer our "Eating Habit Analysis" service—one-on-one consultation with a nutritionist designed to provide a detailed analysis of your current diet and daily eating patterns.

The main goal of such an analysis is not simply to look at what a person eats, but to understand how their diet is structured in real life : which habits already help maintain a balanced diet, where difficulties arise, and what changes can be gradually and comfortably integrated into their usual lifestyle.

A Closer Look at Your Habitual Diet

Each person develops their own nutritional system. Sometimes it develops consciously, and sometimes almost imperceptibly.

Some people start their mornings with a full breakfast, while others limit themselves to a cup of coffee. Some people eat at roughly the same time, while for others, their meals are completely dependent on their work schedule. Some plan their shopping and menus in advance, while others decide what to eat only when they're feeling particularly hungry.

These scenarios themselves aren't strictly divided into "right" and "wrong." What's important is how well the existing nutritional system suits a specific person and their daily routine.

During the analysis, the nutritionist helps you look at your usual diet from a different perspective, identify recurring patterns, and identify areas that require more attention.

This is an opportunity to replace the chaotic attempts to “eat better” with a more understandable and consistent approach.

What does an eating habits analysis include?

The work begins with an examination of the current diet and lifestyle. The nutritionist looks at the overall picture, not just a single meal or a specific day.

The focus may be on:

  • regularity of meals;
  • the usual composition of breakfasts, lunches and dinners;
  • snacks during the day;
  • variety of diet;
  • quantity of vegetables, fruits and other food groups;
  • food preferences;
  • drinking habits;
  • nutritional features on weekdays and weekends;
  • late meals;
  • skipping breakfast or lunch;
  • eating out;
  • choosing products while shopping;
  • the habit of ordering ready-made food;
  • size and structure of usual portions;
  • Everyday situations that influence food choices.

This analysis allows us to see nutrition as a system, rather than as a set of individual decisions.

For example, evening overeating may be related not to dinner itself, but to the fact that the person has eaten almost nothing during the day. Frequent random snacking may occur due to the lack of a proper lunch. A monotonous diet is sometimes the result of a lack of time for shopping and food preparation.

Once the cause becomes clear, it is much easier to work not with its consequences, but with the everyday scenario itself.

Without strict prohibitions and universal templates

We take the approach that recommendations should be realistic, understandable and applicable to everyday life .

An ideal diet that is impossible to follow is rarely beneficial.

If a person has to work late, travel constantly, or spend most of the day away from home, recommendations should take these circumstances into account. If it's not possible to prepare several different meals daily, then simpler solutions are needed. If a person doesn't like certain foods, there's no point in basing their entire diet around them simply because they're considered healthy.

Therefore, analyzing eating habits is not an attempt to force a person to live according to a universal pattern.

Instead, the nutritionist's job is to find solutions that can be integrated into an existing lifestyle with minimal disruption.

Sometimes this can be accomplished by simply changing the structure of a few meals. In other cases, it's worth rethinking your grocery shopping, adding more convenient snack options, or planning meals for particularly busy days.

Small changes that become habits often prove more valuable than large-scale restrictions that you want to abandon after just a few days.

Understanding instead of guilt

One of the important parts of the work is developing a calmer and more conscious attitude towards one’s own nutrition.

Many people evaluate their diet too categorically: “I ate right today,” “I broke down,” “I shouldn’t have eaten that,” “I’m completely cutting out sweets from Monday.”

However, nutrition should not turn into a constant exam.

Analyzing eating habits helps us move from emotional assessments to observation and understanding.

Instead of a question:

"Why didn't I succeed again?"

you can set another one:

“What exactly in my daily routine led to this choice and what can be changed?”

This is a fundamentally different approach. It allows you to avoid beating yourself up over individual decisions and gradually gain a better understanding of your habits and create a more sustainable eating system.

Personalized recommendations for real life

Based on the analysis, the nutritionist formulates recommendations taking into account the individual situation.

They may relate to the structure of the diet, the regularity of meals, the variety of foods, the organization of snacks, shopping planning, and other everyday aspects of nutrition.

Particular attention is paid to ensuring that recommendations are specific .

Not just:

"Eat more variety."

And, for example:

"Add some easy breakfast options that can be prepared in 5-10 minutes."

Not just:

"Don't skip meals."

A:

"Think of a convenient lunch option for those days when you can't leave the office."

Not abstract:

"Eat more vegetables."

And clear ways to add them to familiar dishes without having to completely change the menu.

In this way, recommendations cease to be theory and turn into practical solutions.

Who is suitable for the analysis of eating habits?

This service can be useful for those who want to better understand their own nutrition and make it more organized and conscious.

This analysis is especially relevant if you notice that your nutrition often depends on circumstances: work schedule, fatigue, lack of time, or lack of a pre-planned diet.

It is also suitable for those who:

  • wants to understand the strengths and weaknesses of his current diet;
  • regularly skips meals;
  • often snacks on the go;
  • has difficulties organizing meals on weekdays;
  • wants to make the diet more varied;
  • tired of conflicting nutrition advice;
  • doesn't want to follow strict diets;
  • strives to form a more understandable and sustainable system of eating habits.

You don't have to wait until your diet becomes a serious issue to work with a nutritionist. Sometimes a consultation is needed specifically to understand the current situation and determine the next steps.
